## A Stroll in Durrës and the Hidden Ancient City

A few days ago, I was taking a walk along the beach in Durrës. As I looked out at the sea, a rather curious thought crossed my mind: *how many people have walked along this exact same shoreline before us?*

Thousands of years of history unfolded on this very sand. The funny thing is, if someone had asked me years ago to name a crucial city in the ancient history of this region, I probably wouldn't have even mentioned Durrës. And I would have been completely wrong.

Because beneath the modern city lies the sleeping remains of a far older settlement. The Greeks called it **Epidamnos**.

According to Greek myth, the city was founded by a hero or king named Epidamnos, grandfather of Dyrrhachios. But why found a colony in this specific spot?

When you look at a map, you instantly understand why Greek settlers chose this location around the 7th century BCE:
- From here, it was relatively easy to sail across to Corcyra (modern Corfu).
- Italy lay just across the narrowest pinch of the Adriatic.
- And to the east opened one of the primary natural gateways into the Balkan interior.

You didn't need to be a military genius to realize that whoever controlled this harbor held a massive commercial advantage. Epidamnos was born, quite literally, in the right place at the right time.

## When Roman Superstition Changed a City's Name

Centuries later, the Romans arrived. And here lies a detail that made me smile when I first stumbled upon it.

The Greek name *Epidamnos* suffered from a unfortunate linguistic quirk for Latin speakers. The latter half of the word—*damnos*—sounded uncomfortably close to the Latin verb *damnare*, associated with damnation, ruin, and ill-fortune (*damnum*).

To us today, that might sound absurd. But the Romans were intensely superstitious. Imagine trying to administer one of the Adriatic's busiest commercial ports from a city whose name constantly echoed a curse! It didn't feel like a great omen.

Here's the curious part: the Romans didn't actually invent a new name out of thin air. Dyrrachium had already been circulating among Greek sailors for centuries — it shows up stamped on coins from as far back as the 5th century BCE, long before Rome ever set foot on this coast. It derived from the Greek terms dys (a prefix denoting something difficult, bad, or harsh) and rhachía (a jagged sea-ridge or rocky reef); to sailors, the city was surrounded by a "harsh coastline" or "rough seas." What the Romans actually did was simpler: they just started using that older name more and more, until "Epidamnos" quietly faded away.

Over the centuries, that Latin name evolved into modern-day Durrës. Curiously, the city changed its name, but it never lost its original purpose: it remained one of the most vital strategic ports in the entire Adriatic.

## A Neighborly Feud: Exiled Oligarchs and the Taulantii Kingdom

Up to this point, it sounds like a fairly standard historical narrative. But then, an unexpected crisis erupted.

A bitter political civil war broke out inside Epidamnos. A democratic faction overthrew and expelled the ruling aristocratic oligarchy. The exiled oligarchs fled the city and sought military aid close by.

Surrounding Epidamnos lived some of the region's most powerful Illyrian tribes. Chief among them were the **Taulantii**, a prosperous Illyrian kingdom stretching between modern Durrës and central Albania—covering areas that today include Tirana, Kavajë, and Rrogozhinë.

The exiled oligarchs convinced the Taulantii king to attack the city. Suddenly, Epidamnos found itself under a heavy siege by Illyrian forces.

## When a Local Quarrel Triggers a Global Domino Effect

The desperate democrats inside Epidamnos did what anyone in their shoes would do: they begged for external help.

First, they turned to Corcyra (Corfu), the mother colony from which many original settlers had come. Corcyra refused to intervene.
Desperate, they turned to Corinth, the powerful city-state that had founded Corcyra generations earlier. Corinth accepted.

This was where the domino effect began. Corcyra viewed Corinth’s military intervention in their backyard as a direct threat to their naval dominance. Both city-states mobilized their fleets, and in 433 BCE, they clashed in the massive naval Battle of Sybota off the western coast of Greece.

At that point, it was still a fierce regional conflict. But then, **Athens** entered the picture.

What did the Athenians have to do with any of this? That is precisely the million-dollar question.

Athens had been nervously watching Corinth expand its naval reach. If Corinth defeated Corcyra, it would absorb one of the largest naval fleets in the entire Greek world, completely shattering the balance of power. So, Athens decided to back Corcyra.

And when Athens stepped in, Corinth did what seemed logical: they called upon their ultimate superpower ally, **Sparta**.

In a matter of months, a local political feud born in an Adriatic port town forced the two greatest superpowers of the classical world into a head-on collision. Two years later, the catastrophic Peloponnesian War broke out — the war that the historian Thucydides spent the rest of his life chronicling. He never lived to write its ending; his account breaks off mid-story, in 411 BCE, with Athens still standing. It would take another historian, Xenophon, picking up his pen exactly where Thucydides had put his down, to finally tell us how the Athenian Empire fell.

## Far More Than Just a Port City

That is what blows my mind whenever I walk through Durrës today.

You look at the sea, the beachside cafes, and the waves rolling in. It is hard to wrap your head around the fact that, over two thousand four hundred years ago, this exact shoreline was the catalyst for a chain reaction that reshaped Classical Greece—and when you reshape Classical Greece, you reshape the history of the Western world.

We often fall into the trap of believing history is decided exclusively in grand imperial capitals: Rome, Athens, or Constantinople. But that isn't always true.

Sometimes, all it takes is a small coastal town, a strategically placed harbor, and a terrible political decision to set off events that no one can stop. And Durrës—ancient Epidamnos—was one of those places.
