## The Name We Don't Use: An Example from Chile

I have a personal example that hits very close to home. I am Chilean. If someone asks me where I'm from, I reply *Chile*. If they ask for my nationality, I say *Chilean*. That is how my own people identify themselves.

Yet here in Shqipëri, something curious happens: many people pronounce my country's name as *Kili*. There is no offense intended, nor any malice behind it. It is simply the natural adaptation a language makes when incorporating a foreign word.

However, there is an important distinction: I would never say that I am from *Kili*, nor that my nationality is *Kilian*. That is not the name my own people use to recognize themselves—it is the label another language crafted to refer to us.

It might sound like a trivial detail, but this modern example helps us grasp one of the greatest hurdles when studying the ancient peoples of the Balkans. Time and again, we know an ancient community not by the name they used for themselves, but by the label written down by their neighbors. And that shifts our entire historical perspective.

## A People Without Written Records

Throughout the previous chapters, we have traversed thousands of years of human history. We met early farmers who learned to settle down, witnessed steppe populations arrive and blend with existing Europeans, and observed how metallurgy and Adriatic trade routes connected far-flung communities.

Yet a major problem remains. All of those people left behind tangible artifacts: pottery, weapons, tools, and monumental tombs. What they did not leave behind were books, maps, or written accounts declaring: *“This is who we are.”* And when a people doesn't write its own history, someone else inevitably writes it for them.

## The Arrival of Those Who Could Write

While many Balkan communities continued to preserve and pass down their collective knowledge orally, societies across the Aegean Sea possessed a revolutionary technology: **writing**. The Greeks could record names, places, journeys, and political events.

This gave them an undeniable advantage—not necessarily because they understood foreign societies better, but because they could leave a permanent record of their own interpretation.

Imagine a straightforward scenario: a Greek navigator lands on an unfamiliar coastline. He encounters several distinct communities that speak different dialects, follow different chieftains, and practice different customs. From his external perspective, he needs a convenient label to describe them all. So he uses a single name. That name gets written down. Later, another chronicler adopts it, followed by another. Centuries later, historians discover those documents and repeat the exact same word. That is how a label survives for thousands of years.

## So, Who Were the Illyrians?

This brings us to one of the central questions of this saga. Greek and later Roman authors used the umbrella term *Illyrioi* to describe the diverse populations inhabiting the western Balkans.

However, we must pause. Speaking of "the Illyrians" as a monolith can create a deeply misleading image. We often picture an ancient nation state with an invisible flag, a central capital, and a unified government.

In reality, the situation was far more complex. The region was composed of distinct tribes and independent communities:

- The Taulantii
- The Ardiaei
- The Dardani
- The Dalmatae
- Among many others.

Some formed political alliances, others competed fiercely for territory. Some shared cultural and linguistic traits, while others exhibited profound differences. The overarching term "Illyrians" functioned as an external category to group related peoples, rather than a single political identity embraced by everyone living there.

There is a modern parallel to this that you likely recognize, even if you’ve never thought of it in these terms.

When someone refers to a person from the Netherlands as "Dutch" or calls the country "Holland," they are perpetuating an error the world has repeated for decades. Holland is not the entire nation—Holland consists of just two of the twelve provinces (North Holland and South Holland, home to Amsterdam and Rotterdam). The other ten provinces—Friesland, Limburg, Groningen, and the rest—were never Holland.

Yet the world used "Holland" to represent the whole nation for so long that, in 2020, the Dutch government launched an official rebranding campaign—complete with a new logo—specifically to get people to stop calling the country Holland and use "The Netherlands" instead. Not because "Holland" was entirely false, but because it was a part disguised as the whole.

That is precisely what happened with "Illyrians" two thousand five hundred years earlier, without a government campaign or a new logo to fix it. "Illyrian" was never the native name for everyone. It originally described a specific tribe in the south, near the Adriatic coast. Over time, Greek and Roman writers stretched that label until it covered dozens of distinct tribes who had never called themselves by that name.

The difference is that the Dutch are still here today to correct us. The Illyrians are not.

## The Trap of Modern Lenses

This is one of the most frequent mistakes in historical study: projecting modern concepts onto ancient times. We think in terms of nation-states, fixed borders, and formal nationalities. But a person living 2,500 years ago did not think that way.

Their identity was tied to their immediate family, clan, tribe, valley, or local chieftain. An inhabitant of a village near the Adriatic didn't wake up in the morning thinking: *“I am part of a great Illyrian nation.”* They likely thought: *“I belong to my village, my family, and my land.”* Human identity has always been fluid; we aren't born with eternal labels—we construct them over time.

## History Recorded by External Eyes

This reveals a compelling paradox. We know the Illyrians existed precisely because external societies wrote about them. Without Greek and Roman accounts, we would have far fewer clues to piece together.

At the same time, we must remember that those texts represent an outsider's gaze. It is like having a stranger write a book about your family without ever asking what your family actually calls itself. They might record many accurate details, but they are bound to make mistakes. History works through windows, but no single window offers a complete view of the whole house.

## Setting the Stage for Written History

Up to this point, we have reconstructed a world without written names—a world of farmers, traders, seafarers, metalsmiths, and warriors. Now, for the very first time, those communities enter the written historical record.

It isn't that they suddenly came into existence or didn't exist before. It is simply that someone capable of writing about them finally arrived. And that distinction is fundamental. A society can thrive for thousands of years without ever appearing in a book. Writing doesn't create history—it merely allows certain fragments of it to endure.

In the next chapter, we will see what unfolded when these Adriatic communities came into direct contact with Greek city-states. Coastal ports, thriving trade, military conflicts, and individual names will finally emerge. After thousands of years of silence, the inhabitants of these mountains will step onto the stage of written history.

Yet our initial question will linger: *Are we hearing the true voices of those who lived there... or merely the voices of those who were able to write about them?*
