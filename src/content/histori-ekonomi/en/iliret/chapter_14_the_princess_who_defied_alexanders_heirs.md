Do you remember the princess who defeated an Illyrian queen in hand-to-hand combat when she was just fourteen years old?

That was **Cynane**, half-sister to Alexander the Great. And curiously enough, that teenage victory was by no means the most extraordinary moment of her life.

Her story begins when her father, Philip II, decided to marry Audata, the Illyrian princess with whom he had sealed peace after defeating Bardylis. From that union came Cynane, a child raised between two completely opposite worlds: on one side, the sophisticated Macedonian royal court; on the other, the martial traditions of the Illyrian mountains.

Her mother trained her to ride horses, hunt, and fight. For a woman of the Illyrian nobility, those martial skills were just as essential as knowing how to negotiate a political treaty. Cynane learned well—so well that, while still a teenager, she killed the Illyrian Queen Caeria in single combat.

As was custom among ancient royalty, Philip dictated her marital status. Her husband was Amyntas, a cousin of Alexander who held a legitimate claim to the Macedonian throne. It was not a romantic marriage, but a strategic move to keep a potential rival under tight control. From that union, a daughter named **Adea** was born.

It seemed like the beginning of a quiet life, but in 336 BCE, Philip II was assassinated. Alexander inherited the kingdom and, to secure his grip on power, purged all potential claimants to the throne. Among those executed was Amyntas.

Almost overnight, Cynane was left a widow with a young daughter to raise alone.

Sometime later, Alexander attempted to arrange another marriage for her. The chosen suitor was Langarus, king of the Agrianians, who had aided Alexander during the Pelium campaign. But fate intervened once again: Langarus fell suddenly ill and died just before the wedding could take place.

Two marriages ruined before Cynane was even in her mid-twenties.

It was then that she made a crucial choice: she stopped waiting for others to decide her fate. Instead of seeking another husband, she trained her daughter Adea exactly as Audata had trained her. She taught her how to ride, wield weapons, and command troops in the field. She wasn't raising a passive court lady; she was forging a military leader.

## Defying Alexander's Generals

Years passed. Alexander conquered the Persian Empire, pushed all the way to India, and built the largest empire the world had ever seen. But in 323 BCE, he died unexpectedly in Babylon without leaving an adult heir to inherit his crown.

Instantly, the million-dollar question emerged: *who would take control?*

Alexander’s generals proclaimed his half-brother, Philip III Arrhidaeus, as king—a man who suffered from severe intellectual disabilities. Everyone knew that real power would rest in the hands of whoever controlled the monarch.

For Cynane, now around 34 years old, this was the ultimate opportunity. If she could marry Adea to the new king, her daughter would become queen of the empire. Without asking permission from any of the warring generals, she raised a private army and marched straight into Asia.

The regent of the empire, Perdiccas, immediately recognized the threat. He dispatched his own brother, General Alcetas, with an army to intercept her at Sardis.

What happened there was dramatic. The Macedonian soldiers sent to block her way recognized Cynane instantly. She was Philip II’s daughter, Alexander’s sister. Many hesitated to draw weapons against her. Cynane stepped forward and delivered a fiery speech, reminding them of her royal bloodline. Realizing he was losing control of his men, Alcetas executed her on the spot in the middle of the field.

It seemed like the end. In reality, it was the fuse that lit the powder keg.

## Victory Beyond Death

Cynane’s murder sparked widespread outrage among the Macedonian soldiers. Executing Philip II’s daughter was an unpardonable political mistake. The military backlash was so intense that the regent Perdiccas had no choice but to back down and authorize the royal marriage.

Adea married King Philip III and assumed the royal name **Eurydice**. Cynane, even in death, had won her final battle.

With her husband incapable of ruling, Eurydice took on an increasingly assertive political and military role. For a brief window in 317 BCE, she exercised effective, sole rule over Macedon.

Decades earlier, the Greeks had dismissed the Illyrians as "barbarians" simply because of their language. Now, the granddaughter of an Illyrian princess occupied the center of power in the Macedonian kingdom.

Her reign was short-lived. Olympias, Alexander the Great's formidable mother, returned from exile to claim power on behalf of her infant grandson. Eurydice was captured and offered three choices for her execution: a sword, a rope, or a cup of poison. True to her unyielding character, she chose the rope and hanged herself.

Years later, when General Cassander secured control, he ordered King Philip III and Eurydice to be buried with full royal honors. In that very same ceremony, Cynane’s remains were brought to rest alongside them. Mother and daughter ended up resting together for eternity.

It all started with a peace treaty marriage between Philip II and the Illyrian princess Audata. Years later, that very same Illyrian bloodline sat, if only for a brief moment, upon the throne of the greatest empire of its time.
