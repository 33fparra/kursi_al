When we hear the word *barbarian* today, almost all of us picture the exact same thing: a savage people, violent, uncultured, someone completely devoid of civilization.

But that was not its original meaning.

The word was born in ancient Greece. The Greeks called those who spoke languages they couldn’t understand *barbaros*. To their ears, those foreign languages sounded like incomprehensible babble, something akin to *bar... bar... bar...*. The term was literally born from linguistic mockery.

At first, it wasn't even an insult. It simply meant "foreigner" or "one who does not speak Greek." It was the passing of centuries, especially during the Roman era, that eventually turned "barbarian" into a synonym for someone backward or savage.

And that distinction matters a lot.

Because when we read that Alexander the Great fought against the "northern barbarians," our imagination tends to fill in the blanks with images that belong more to Hollywood than to real history.

The Illyrians were not disorganized hordes running down from mountains screaming war cries. For decades, they had proven to be a formidable enemy. They had repeatedly defeated Macedon, they had forced Philip II to completely reinvent his army, and for years, they had been the single biggest geopolitical headache for Macedonian kings.

Now, it was Alexander's turn to face Bardylis's son.

## A Kingdom Without a King

In 336 BCE, Macedon was paralyzed by unexpected news.

Philip II had been assassinated during the celebration of his daughter’s wedding. The man who had transformed a fragile border kingdom into the dominant military power in the region fell not on a battlefield, but during a public ceremony.

His heir was barely twenty years old.

And many thought that this young man would never measure up to his father.

Greek city-states began to question Macedonian authority. Up north, several peoples saw the perfect opportunity to regain the independence Philip had stripped from them.

Among them appeared an old acquaintance: **Cleitus**, son of the Illyrian king Bardylis.

The very same Bardylis who for more than thirty years had humiliated Macedon and forced Philip to build the most advanced army of his time. Now, it was his son's turn to test the strength of Philip's successor.

## Alexander's First Test

It is hard not to see a curious historical irony here.

Bardylis forced Philip to create a completely new army. Decades later, his son Cleitus would be the first major enemy Alexander had to defeat to prove he was truly worthy of commanding that army.

Without meaning to, father and son were present at the two moments that made the Macedonian Empire possible: one forced the birth of the military machine; the other tested whether that machine worked even without Philip at the helm.

And Cleitus was not alone.

He achieved something that rarely happened among Illyrian peoples: several tribes put aside their differences and formed a powerful coalition. Among them were the Taulantii, led by King Glaukias—the very same tribe we met during the Epidamnos conflict. Cleitus tried to expand the coalition even further by bringing in the Autariatae, but Alexander reacted swiftly: he convinced his ally Langarus, king of the Agrianians, to attack them before they could join forces.

For the first time since inheriting the throne, Alexander found himself facing an army fully capable of destroying him.

And if he fell there, his dream of conquering Asia would die in that mountain valley.

## The Battle of Pelium

The clash took place near Pelium, in a rugged mountainous region where narrow passes turned any tactical maneuver into a logistical nightmare.

It was exactly the kind of terrain that favored the Illyrians.

The ancient historian Arrian, our primary source for this campaign, records that before the combat, the Illyrians performed a grim ritual sacrifice: three boys, three girls, and three black rams. This specific detail underscores the immense gravity both sides placed on this decisive confrontation.

And for a few terrifying moments, the encirclement grew so tight that Macedon seemed genuinely doomed.

Alexander had advanced too deep into the pass.
The mountains blocked any frontal assault.
Fresh Illyrian forces were appearing from the rear.
To attack meant slaughter.
To retreat through those narrow gorges meant total destruction.

For the first time in his life, the young king was completely trapped.

## The Army That Fought as One Man

Where any other general would have ordered a chaotic retreat or a desperate charge, Alexander executed a stroke of psychological genius.

He formed the phalanx and ordered them to execute their training drills in absolute, eerie silence.

Thousands of soldiers began moving in perfect, unbroken unison.
The massive *sarissas* were raised.
They were lowered.
They swung left and right in formation.

All executed with a degree of military discipline almost impossible to comprehend for that era.

From the high slopes, the Illyrian warriors halted their pressure to watch this strange, mesmerizing display. It wasn’t an attack. It wasn’t a retreat. It was as if a single giant organism were maneuvering thousands of bronze spearheads with surgical precision.

That tactic produced exactly what Alexander intended: total distraction. For a few critical minutes, the Illyrians lost their tactical focus, mesmerized by the human machine below.

That tiny window of time was all Alexander needed.

The Companion cavalry (*Hetairoi*) immediately charged in a wedge formation with devastating force.

The silence shattered under the thunder of hooves. The Macedonians broke the encirclement, prompting a hasty and disorganized retreat among the Illyrian forces.

Days later, Alexander launched a surprise night attack, definitively crushing Cleitus's coalition. His northern frontier was finally secure.

## The "Barbarians" Who Joined Alexander's Army

Without that tactical victory at Pelium, the grand campaign into Asia would never have launched.

But the most compelling conclusion to this story came afterward.

Once peace was established, many of the very same peoples the Greeks labeled "barbarians" were integrated among the most valued troops of the Macedonian army. Numerous Illyrian warriors—renowned for their toughness and mountain warfare expertise—served faithfully throughout Alexander's campaigns in Asia.

There is a profound historical irony in all of this.

The Greeks had invented a word to visually and culturally divide "themselves" from "the others."

Yet Alexander cared far less about how a soldier's language sounded than about his ability to hold the line and stand firm when the battle began.

Perhaps that is one of the greatest lessons this episode leaves us: words and labels can survive for centuries, but military and human reality is always far more complex than the labels we use to simplify it.

And as Alexander prepared to cross the Hellespont to alter world history, those northern soldiers once dismissed as barbarians marched right alongside him.
