## Who Actually Won the Feud of Durrës?

So, who actually won in the end? The democrats of Durrës or its expelled oligarchs?

Ironically, neither.

As Corinth, Corcyra, Athens, and Sparta collided in what we now know as the Peloponnesian War, the original local feud in the port of Epidamnos quickly ceased to matter. The expelled aristocrats never truly regained firm control of the city as they had originally envisioned. Epidamnos simply became a minor pawn on a vastly larger geopolitical chessboard.

It is curious how history works. Sometimes a petty local dispute triggers a massive wildfire, and by the time the storm passes, hardly anyone remembers what started the blaze in the first place.

When the Peloponnesian War finally concluded, Classical Greece emerged battered and exhausted. Athens had lost its empire. Sparta failed to maintain hegemony for long. While Greek city-states remained wealthy and culturally brilliant, politically they were deeply fractured.

Along the coastline, other colonies like **Apollonia**—founded by Corinthian and Corcyrean settlers near the Vjosa River—continued to flourish as bustling commercial hubs. But while the Greeks looked outward toward maritime trade, something transformative was brewing in the Balkan interior.

The tribes inhabiting vast stretches of modern-day Shqipëri (Albania), Kosovo, Montenegro, and surrounding regions began organizing in an entirely new way. It was no longer enough for individual communities to handle their own isolated problems.

The world was changing. And they adapted right alongside it.

## Success Brings New Problems

As we've seen in earlier chapters, when there is nothing worth stealing, nobody bothers to build massive stone ramparts. But when a society prospers, protecting its wealth becomes just as vital as acquiring it. And defending that wealth requires far more than skilled farmers.

It demands someone capable of organizing.  
Of negotiating alliances.  
And, when necessary, of leading an army into battle.

## When a Chieftain Becomes More Than a Chieftain

For centuries, tribal leaders ruled relatively small, localized clans. But a few individuals began accomplishing something far more challenging: **convincing multiple independent tribes to act as one**.

Not because everyone held identical beliefs or shared a unified national identity—that modern concept did not exist yet. They joined forces because unified tribes stood a far better chance of survival than isolated ones.

It is a pattern repeated throughout human history: first comes a coalition, then someone emerges to make the critical decisions. Over time, that temporary wartime leadership becomes permanent. And ultimately, a **kingdom** is born.

## The First Great Illyrian King: Bardylis

It is within this high-stakes environment that one of the most towering figures of ancient Balkan history enters the stage: **Bardylis**.

Ancient chroniclers write about him, though far less than we might wish. We know precious little about his youth, and later Roman anecdotes even claim he worked as a humble charcoal burner before rising to royal power. While it makes for a colorful legend, historians cannot verify his origins with certainty.

What we do know for certain is the impressive length of his reign. By the time this story reaches its climax, Bardylis had been ruling for over thirty years—longer than most neighboring kings had even been alive.

Under his command, diverse Illyrian tribes acted with a level of strategic coordination never seen before. For the first time, the Kingdom of Macedon could no longer look to its northern frontier with peace of mind. They now faced a neighbor fully capable of defeating them.

## The Day Macedon Learned Fear

Today, we know ancient Macedon primarily through the legendary conquest of Alexander the Great. But during this era, Alexander wasn't even born yet.

Long before Alexander, Bardylis's Illyrians repeatedly shattered Macedonian armies in open battle. And they didn't win through sheer numbers or wild ferocity alone. Bardylis’s forces had adopted the heavy equipment and battlefield tactics of the Greek hoplite—large shields, long spears, and tight phalanx formations—even before the Macedonians themselves did. They were no chaotic mountain hoard; in many respects, they fielded the most formidable army in the region.

In 359 BCE, that military superiority delivered a devastating blow. **Perdiccas III**, the King of Macedon himself, fell in battle against Bardylis. Alongside their king, four thousand Macedonian soldiers were slaughtered in a single afternoon.

It was one of the most catastrophic defeats Macedon would ever suffer. The disaster was so profound that the historian Diodorus Siculus recorded that the surviving Macedonians were too terrified to continue fighting.

For years, Macedon was forced to accept a humiliating reality: the most dangerous kingdom on their border wasn't the Persian Empire. **It was Iliria**. And Bardylis regularly exacted heavy financial tribute from them.

## A Young Prince Watching from the South

While Bardylis consolidated his grip and maintained financial dominance over Macedon, a young Macedonian prince was learning a lesson he would never forget. And he didn't learn it from a distance.

Around 369 BCE, when he was just thirteen years old, a clash between his older brother Alexander II and the Illyrians resulted in Bardylis taking the young prince hostage for a brief period. While ancient sources offer sparse details on his captivity, the historical irony is undeniable: the very Illyrian monarch this prince would face decades later as king had once held him as a child prisoner.

He had watched his kingdom suffer humiliating defeats. He had seen his brother, King Perdiccas III, die on the battlefield. And he understood that if Macedon was to survive, it had to solve its existential problem in the north.

That prince's name was **Philip II**.

Very soon, he would revolutionize the Macedonian army. And, without knowing it yet, he would alter the course of world history—setting the stage for an inevitable showdown against a warlord who, by then, would be over ninety years old and had no intention of backing down.
