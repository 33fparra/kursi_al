## The Legend of the Lost City

Back in South America, where I come from, there are countless stories about lost cities. Perhaps the most famous is El Dorado, a city that, according to legend, was overflowing with gold and unimaginable wealth hidden somewhere deep in the jungle. For centuries, explorers blew entire fortunes trying to find it. It never appeared.

But the funny thing is, that concept doesn’t just belong to the Americas. The Balkans have their very own lost city. And, unlike El Dorado, this one actually existed. The problem is that, after more than two thousand four hundred years, we still don't know exactly where it was.

Its name was **Damastion**.

## A War That Ended Up Creating a City

Remember when we talked about Epidamnos? A dispute between the inhabitants of that small coastal town ended up dragging in Corinth, Corcyra, Athens, and Sparta. What began as a local feud ultimately snowballed into one of the main causes of the Peloponnesian War.

Wars destroy cities. But they also do something else: they force thousands of people to start over, often far away from where they began.

That exact war, just as it was starting, was already claiming very specific victims. In 431 BCE, Athens expelled the entire population of the island of Aegina and repopulated it with its own settlers; the exiled Aeginetans had no choice but to rebuild their lives elsewhere. Years later, in 423 BCE, it was Mende's turn, a city that had rebelled against Athens and paid for that rebellion with ruthless suppression.

Two punished cities. Hundreds of homeless families.

And according to many historians, some of those families ended up trekking hundreds of kilometers north, eventually founding an entirely new city right in the middle of the Balkan mountains—far from any sea, and even farther from any Athenian army.

And here lies something truly strange. The Greeks almost always founded their colonies by the sea. It makes perfect sense: the sea allowed them to trade, fish, receive reinforcements, and maintain contact with their mother city.

Damastion completely broke that rule. It wasn't born facing the Adriatic. It was born facing a mountain.

Why? Because buried inside that mountain was something capable of changing the destiny of an entire city: **silver**.

## A City Built on Wealth... And the Proof Stamped on Coins

Today, we tend to associate wealth with bank accounts, stock portfolios, or corporations. In the 4th century BCE, a silver mine could be worth far more than all of that. Silver allowed a city to mint coins, and coins allowed a state to pay soldiers, buy goods, finance massive projects, and grow.

Damastion prospered precisely because of that resource. And this is where the story gets really interesting. Someone might ask: if the city is lost, how are we so sure it even existed, let alone what its exact name was?

The answer lies in the clearest physical evidence possible: **its coins**.

For decades, archaeologists have found hundreds of ancient silver coins scattered across the region. And there is absolutely no doubt about where they came from, because those coins literally have the city's name engraved on them in Greek letters: «ΔΑΜΑΣΤΙΝΩΝ» (*Damastinon*), meaning "of the Damastini" or "of the people of Damastion". It is the official signature of the city stamped into metal.

But those coins tell us something else when we look closely. On one side, they bear the head of the Greek god Apollo. On the flip side, they feature a sacred tripod, wine cups (kantharos), or even mining tools like pickaxes.

To the people of that era, this sent a crystal-clear message. Damastion was not just an isolated mining pit in the mountains, nor was it a simple military camp. It was surrounded by Illyrian and Balkan tribes, yet it maintained a strong Greek culture. Everything points toward exiled Greek colonists and local Illyrian inhabitants finding a way to work together instead of spending their lives at war: in places like Damastion, Greek artisans likely brought the technical know-how to design the coins and smelt the silver, while the Illyrian tribes controlled the rugged territory and, most probably, supplied the workforce and protected the mine.

Their coins began circulating throughout a massive chunk of the Balkans: from modern-day Kosovo and southern Serbia, all the way down to North Macedonia and the Adriatic coast. Every coin that changed hands was physical proof of a commercial web linking the high mountains to the sea.

## The City Everyone Knows... But Nobody Can Find

And this is where the story takes an almost absurd turn.

We know Damastion existed. We have the silver coins engraved with the city's exact name, we know it exploited massive mines, and we know it was important enough for the famous ancient geographer Strabo to mention it in his writings centuries later. But nobody has been able to prove exactly where on the map it was built.

Some researchers believe it was located near Artana (Novobërdë) in Kosovo, an area historically famous for its rich mineral deposits. Others place it in the Kopaonik region, between Serbia and Kosovo. There are also hypotheses pointing toward northern Macedonia or even southern Shqipëri.

It is baffling: we have the money, we have the ancient texts, and we have the mines. The only thing we still can't find are the actual walls and houses of the city.

## The Silver That Changed History

The most fascinating part is how this story intersects with the rise of Macedon.

While Bardylis was trying to hold his Illyrian kingdom together, Philip II was busy transforming Macedon into the most efficient military machine of its time. All of that cost money: training professional soldiers, forging thousands of massive *sarissa* spears, producing armor, and maintaining a standing army was astronomically expensive.

And then something happened that probably no one in Damastion could have foreseen. Shortly after crushing Bardylis in the Erigon Valley, Macedon seized control of the mountainous region where those silver mines were located. Many historians consider it highly probable that the wealth extracted from Damastion's mines went directly toward paying for Philip's military reforms and funding his soldiers' wages.

If that is true, the historical irony is staggering: a portion of the wealth generated in territory controlled for years by the Illyrians ended up financing the very Macedonian army that consolidated Philip's power and, years later, allowed his son Alexander the Great to conquer the Persian Empire.

History has a rather peculiar sense of humor.

## Cities Can Disappear Too

When we think of a major city, we usually assume it will always leave a gigantic footprint that will last forever. Rome is still here. Athens is still here. Durrës is still looking out over the Adriatic after more than two thousand six hundred years.

Damastion wasn't so lucky.

Its wealth dried up or was seized by others.
Its inhabitants scattered.
Its exact location was swallowed by time.

All that was left were some silver coins carrying its name, traveling from hand to hand... and a few lines written in ancient books.

Sometimes, that is all that survives of a city.
